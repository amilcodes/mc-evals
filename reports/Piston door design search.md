# Turn 36,000 doors an hour into leverage

The Codex chat got the direction right on most of its calls: publish Piston Door Bench as a research eval, don't try to sell it as a standalone eval, treat repair as the environment framing, prefer RoboGrammar's macro-moves to Repulsive Shells' geometry, borrow EDA's verification discipline, and don't make Verilog the spine. It missed the fact that should drive the plan. Your simulator grades a small door in about 0.1 s, roughly **36,000 evaluations an hour on one process**. That makes one hour of brute-force evaluation worth about **80 times every test call all 58 agent runs made combined** ([internal timing notes](<../runs/bench/search_pilot/timing.json>); [runs.json](<../runs/bench/dashboard/runs.json>)). At that price, the question isn't "LLM reasoning or optimizer?" Treat the agents' doors as seeds and frontier priors, treat cheap search as the workhorse, and treat the head-to-head between them as your most interesting eval result. Your friend's "invalid designs are infinitely far away" intuition, taken literally, is the wrong default here. A hard barrier on a discrete design graph confines search to the connected patch of working doors around the seed. STOKE showed that a strict pass/fail objective performs barely better than random search, while a graded "almost correct" score is what makes search work ([STOKE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0557)). Your delay sweep already shows how sparse the working set is: only **3 of 16** repeater settings on the Fable 70 work, though one of them opens in 30 ticks and closes in 18, against the original's 34 and 22, at the same volume of 70. The most informative next experiment is an afternoon-scale census of every single-block edit around the Fable 70 and the Opus 240. It tells you whether working doors form a connected network you can walk, whether a graded trace score points toward valid designs, and therefore which search method to build. Everything else in this report is sequenced behind that measurement.

A note on sources: links to `grader/` and `runs/` files point into your project. Links to `runs/bench/search_pilot/` point to read-only measurements a researcher ran against your grader on 16 November. Web links are papers and pages the researchers read. Statements framed as inference or recommendation are reasoning, not sourced fact.

## Publish the eval, and include a search baseline in it

**Where Codex was right.** Its ranking (publish now, sellable eval weak, RL environment possible only through repair and adaptation) matches the market evidence. Epoch's interviews with 18 people in the field found that labs request RL environments roughly **10 to 20 times more often than benchmarks**. Buyers rank soundness first ("high reward must mean the task was actually solved, not hacked") and want pass rates above zero but below about 70% ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/state-of-rl-envs)). A real game engine as the grader is about as sound as reward gets, and door size gives a natural difficulty ladder. Codex was also right that the deciding result for any sale is transfer: a model trained on door repair beating its base model on an unseen mechanism.

**Where it was shallow.** First, it described what the vendors sell without the economics that decide whether you fit. Mechanize argues that spending under about $500 per task wastes RL compute and expects prices to reach "a few thousand dollars per task," built by experts rather than generated procedurally ([Mechanize](https://www.mechanize.work/blog/cheap-rl-tasks-will-waste-compute/)). Reported prices run **$200 to $2,000 per task** and about **$20K for a website-clone environment** ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/state-of-rl-envs)). Games don't appear on Epoch's demand map at all. Scale already ships a Minecraft plugin inside its open AgentEnv framework ([Scale AgentEnv](https://labs.scale.com/blog/introducing-agentenv)). Commercial game environments exist (Good Start Labs, Arkadium's GameLab), but no lab has publicly bought an academic game benchmark. The money in this niche comes from services and reputation, as with AI Diplomacy leading to Good Start Labs. Second, the "tinier labs" correction matters: Tinker and the Prime Intellect hub are a free route to making the environment *trainable* by smaller labs. Prime Intellect's bounties run about **$100 to $5K** ([Prime Intellect](https://primeintellect.ai/blog/scaling-environments-program)), which is the realistic price floor for an open release.

Third, and most important, Codex never asked what your simulator's speed does to a repair environment. The Fable 70's repeater delays break the door in 13 of 16 settings. Every one of those is a "repair" task an agent with a Python session can solve by enumerating all 16 settings in about **1.5 seconds** ([timing.json](<../runs/bench/search_pilot/timing.json>)). Any repair whose fix lies one edit away is solvable by brute force over the neighborhood. That tests whether the agent thinks to run a search, not spatial reasoning. Hard repair tasks therefore need fixes that are several edits deep. The physics port is the natural one: the vanilla Fable 70 under the combined change, and the Opus changed-physics 70 under vanilla, both fail at every delay setting and need structural edits. Alternatively, cap simulator calls tightly.

Codex's "hidden rule change" idea, which it called the strongest research question, is weaker than it sounds. Your physics agent can patch only **four integer constants**: repeater unit, push limit, observer delay and observer pulse ([PhysicsAgent.java](<../grader/kernel/agent/pd/PhysicsAgent.java>)). A competent agent identifies any of them with one lab experiment, such as placing a repeater and stepping ticks. Held-out "physics families" built from four knobs also give little diversity for an RL buyer.

**Concurrent work sharpens the positioning.** RedstoneBench is live, with 16 tasks including 2×2, 3×3 and 4×4 piston doors on a vanilla server, volume scoring, and 40 tries within an hour. Its leaderboard so far reports only early 2×2 results, with a best 2×2 volume of 16 ([RedstoneBench](https://redstone-bench.adamholter.chatgpt.site/)). Your 2×2 doors sit at 30. The gap almost certainly reflects your stricter rules (seamless hallway visibility, eight cycles with idle delays, 11-placement robustness), not weaker builds, but it is an inference you should state explicitly when you publish. SciCrafter covers lamp-pattern redstone with no pistons, where frontier models plateau near 26% ([SciCrafter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.24697)).

**What to believe and do.** Your defensible differences are pistons under strict community rules, changed physics, tick-trace tooling, and something nobody else has: **a measured comparison of frontier agents against cheap search at an equal simulator budget**. Put that comparison in the paper. Agents got 150 test calls per run, so the benchmark's budgets made the bitter-lesson strategy (write a search loop and let it run) impossible. A second track that gives agents a compute budget, measured in simulator-seconds instead of test calls, would measure whether a model knows how to spend compute. That question interests labs more than "can it build a door." Keep the final grade on an isolated referee, as the earlier design required. Release the repair variant openly in a hub-compatible format. Treat selling it as conditional on a transfer result, not as a plan.

## Repulsive Shells transfers as margins, not as a wall

**Where Codex was right.** After several rounds, it landed on the correct core: the useful analogue of repulsion is a **shrinking timing margin**, and edits that jump straight from working to broken should simply be tried and rejected. Its table of "A clears at tick 8, B pushes at tick 10" margins is the right picture, and its suggestion to try paired edits is sound.

**Where it was wrong or too shallow.** It first overstated the connection, then retreated too far, ending with "you can search for faster doors using that check alone." That retreat discards the most useful idea in the thread. It also told you the paper "searches for a motion" while you search for a design. The project page also shows *static* design optimization, packing shapes into containers ([Repulsive Shells](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~kmcrane/Projects/RepulsiveShells/index.html)).

The precise discrete translation is this. Your design space is a graph: doors are nodes and edits are edges. "Invalid designs are infinitely far away" means assigning infinite cost to every broken door. That is the death penalty, and under it a search can only ever walk the connected cluster of working doors that contains its seed. The continuous shape space in the paper is path-connected, so its barrier never blocks a good destination. Door space carries no such guarantee. STOKE's x86 search found that expert code sat in "an entirely different region of the space," reachable only by building the new code next to the old while cost *rises*, then deleting the original. Its strict correctness metric ended "only slightly superior to … purely random search," while a graded Hamming score that gave credit for almost-correct outputs succeeded ([STOKE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1211.0557)). The volume objective makes this worse for doors. A 7×10×1 box shrinks only when an entire boundary row or column empties, so every shrinking move is a multi-edit move whose intermediate states are no smaller.

What to keep from the paper is its real mechanism, cost that rises smoothly as you approach failure, used in two places. Inside the working set, use slack to rank and steer. Temporal robustness is how many ticks each piston event could shift before the door fails, the discrete version of Donzé and Maler's robustness semantics for temporal logic. Edit robustness is the fraction of single edits that keep the door working, Wagner's mutational robustness ([Wagner 2008](https://www.ieu.uzh.ch/wagner/papers/Wagner_PRS08.pdf)). Placement robustness is the 11-placement pass rate you already compute. Outside the working set, replace the infinite wall with a graded distance. Your kernel already emits the doorway pattern every tick ([PdKernel.java](<../grader/kernel/src/pd/PdKernel.java>)), so a Hamming distance between the observed and target aperture over all ticks of all flips is a STOKE-style "almost correct" score you can compute today. Expect STOKE's documented failure mode: broken doors that differ from working ones by a single block or a single tick are deceptive, so score per tick, not just the final state.

The friend's idea does have one literal home that Codex brushed past: the **motion layer**. If you abstract a door as a schedule of piston actions on a voxel grid, choosing the schedule that takes the doorway from closed to open without moving blocks colliding is a genuine path-planning problem between two fixed endpoints. That is exactly Repulsive Shells' setting. It belongs in the layered representation described under the Verilog line below.

## RoboGrammar's value is macro-moves mined from your 58 doors

**Where Codex was right.** RoboGrammar is the closer fit, and Codex identified why: a rule places a whole wiring pattern at once, so a search can make a coordinated change that would break the door if installed block by block. That is the macro-move fix for STOKE's "low-probability path" problem.

**Where it was shallow.** RoboGrammar's grammar guarantees that designs are *buildable*, not that they *work*. The paper says the framework "ensures that simulated designs are at least fabricable," and still handles self-collision by rejection and by blocking partial designs after 100 failed completions. It also warns that giving failures a fixed low score "unnecessarily biases the search towards simpler designs" ([RoboGrammar](https://people.csail.mit.edu/jiex/papers/robogrammar/paper.pdf)). Its learned value function exists because each evaluation was expensive: 2,000 iterations took about 31 hours, about 20 of them in simulation. That works out to about 36 seconds of simulation per design. Yours take about 0.1 s, a few hundred times cheaper, so for small grammars you can enumerate where RoboGrammar had to learn. Codex also proposed swapping the Fable 70's timing circuit without checking that it *has* a separable timing circuit. The door is 47 blocks in one layer with 2 repeaters, and nobody has ablated it to learn which blocks are necessary ([blocks.py](<../runs/bench/search_pilot/blocks.py>)).

The deeper caveat comes from EDA. Minimal doors win by **sharing across module boundaries**: an observer doing two jobs, a piston that also acts as wiring. A grammar of clean modules biases search toward modular, larger designs. Use the grammar for feasibility, for seeding, and for the 4×4 rung, where the best door has 10 repeaters and 118 blocks. Then follow it with a merge-and-rewrite pass, the way ABC rewrites logic after technology mapping.

**What to do.** Don't hand-author the grammar. Mine it: ablate each of your 58 verified doors, cut out the sub-assemblies that survive as units, and characterize each one (footprint, ports, tick delay, neighbours that break it). The closest evidence for how to search over them is Minecraft-specific. On piston-and-slime flying machines, MAP-Elites with a descriptor that counted **piston orientations** succeeded in **70% and 93% of 30 runs**, against 30% and 47% for fitness alone, and size-based descriptors did no better than fitness ([Medina et al., GECCO 2023](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2302.00782)). For doors, the archive descriptors should therefore describe mechanism type (piston count and orientation, observer versus repeater timing, number of timing channels), with volume and ticks as the objectives inside each cell.

## EDA is being bitter-lessoned in the outer loop, and your economics are inverted

**Where Codex was right.** Its core claim holds: frontier models propose useful changes, and the shipped results are verifier-gated. AlphaEvolve's Verilog rewrite went into a TPU only after it "had to pass robust verification methods" ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/alphaevolve-a-gemini-powered-coding-agent-for-designing-advanced-algorithms/)). Its "steal" list is sound: compact failure reports, an editable layer above raw blocks, and cheap screening before full verification.

**Where it was too shallow.** "Consistent with the Bitter Lesson" hides what is actually being displaced. Learning and search are replacing EDA's *human-written outer loop*: flow scripts, parameter tuning, rewrite-sequence choice, and even solver heuristics. An LLM-evolved heuristic won the main track of SAT Competition 2025, by its authors' account ([AESAT, IJCAI 2026](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/257)). The exact engines are not being replaced. AlphaChip's learned placement remains disputed in independent replication, which uses strong simulated annealing and commercial baselines ([Cheng, Kahng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.11014v3)). The verifiers are the reward signal, so better models make them more valuable, not less. Codex also treated FluxBench's 98-vs-12 as strong evidence. It comes from a single run per cell on a benchmark written by the company that sells the winning harness. The same table shows Claude Sonnet 5 *dropping* from 87 to 12 when skills were added, and GLM-5.2 with skills scoring 96 ([FluxBench](https://fluxbench.net)). The ICLAD study is cleaner, and its sharpest number is one Codex skipped: coding agents without the verify loop averaged **0 to 5%** Fmax gain, against about 20% with it ([ICLAD 2026](https://renau.github.io/docs/iclad26.pdf)).

**The point Codex missed entirely: your cost structure is the reverse of EDA's.** In the ICLAD loop, synthesis and equivalence checking take about **75% of wall-clock time** and the LLM about 26%, so model proposals are relatively cheap and every verifier call is precious ([ICLAD 2026](https://renau.github.io/docs/iclad26.pdf)). In your loop the verifier takes about 0.1 s, while one agent proposal takes minutes and dollars. The Fable 70 took 22 tests over a 120-minute, $47 run ([runs.json](<../runs/bench/dashboard/runs.json>)). That puts doors closer to STOKE, which evaluated more than 50,000 proposals per second, than to chip design. The February 2026 "simple baselines" study reinforces this. Plain repeated sampling matched or beat a full evolutionary framework on most AlphaEvolve math problems, and reformulating the search space improved *every* method, which led the authors to conclude that "the primary challenge … is designing good search spaces" ([arXiv 2602.16805](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16805v1)).

**What to borrow, concretely.** Borrow the **evaluation cascade**. Your grader always simulates every cycle even after the first flip fails, and pads each flip with at least about 60 fixed ticks ([grade.py](<../grader/pdgrader/grade.py>)). A screening mode (abort on first failure, one cycle, shorter waits), then dev mode, then final mode plus 11 placements, should cut the cost of a failing candidate by an estimated 2 to 3 times. Borrow **gate on validity, rank on cost**, the "equivalence-checked, frequency-scored" pattern. Borrow a **tick-level timing report**: per-piston arrival tick, slack against the deadline, and same-tick race warnings. That is static timing analysis translated into ticks and the localization signal both agents and search need. Borrow **coarse-but-correct localization**: ICLAD found that an accurate coarse locator beat a precise probabilistic one ([ICLAD 2026](https://renau.github.io/docs/iclad26.pdf)). Borrow **DRiLLS's split**, where the learner chooses the next verified transformation and the tool performs it ([DRiLLS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.04021)). Borrow **multiple seeds per cell**, because both EDA studies are single runs per cell. Later, consider SATLUTION's move of letting an LLM evolve the *harness's own* heuristics, such as candidate ranking.

## Verilog belongs nowhere near the inner loop

**Where Codex was right.** Verilog-to-redstone flows exist: Dewey, RTL2MC, V2MC, MinecraftHDL, RHDL ([Dewey](https://github.com/qmn/dewey); [RTL2MC](https://github.com/NTP17/rtl2mc)). Verilator simulates the RTL model, not Minecraft. "Two circuits can agree on outputs while one jams" is the decisive objection, and a tiny Minecraft-specific representation for one swappable timing module is the right first step.

**Where it was shallow.** It didn't say what that representation should be, and it didn't answer Raymond's question about whether redstone components map one-to-one onto logic gates. They don't, for reasons no HDL flow models. **None of the flows found handle pistons or timing-driven sequencing.** The only formal verification project proves steady-state behaviour of acyclic circuits and says timing "is not proved in general" ([redstone-verify](https://github.com/formalized-si/redstone-verify)). Pistons act through block events in their own phase of each tick. Ordering inside a tick depends on scheduled-tick priorities and insertion order, and Java has no engine-level "redstone tick" at all ([Minecraft Wiki: Tick](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Tick)). Short pulses change piston outcomes, and pistons rewrite the circuit by moving its conductors. This is also why MCHPRS's fast compiler excludes pistons: it compiles a fixed connectivity graph ([Redpiler](https://github.com/MCHPR/MCHPRS/blob/master/docs/Redpiler.md)). Verilator would certify the controller's waveform while the jam happens in the mechanism.

**What to believe.** You already own the thing Raymond's pipeline would end in: a fast, exact oracle running Mojang's unmodified 26.3 code. The IR to build sits *above* it, in three layers. The first is a **motion schedule**: piston actions with a tick and an order within the tick, plus pushability preconditions. This is where Repulsive Shells' collision-free path idea applies literally. The second is a **timed signal graph**: repeaters, observers and dust with exact delays, where edges can depend on geometry ("this dust exists only while that block is unmoved"). The third is the **block embedding**, where volume is decided. Each layer has its own exact tool. The SAT-redstone "one copy of the variables per scenario" trick extends to one copy per tick for small bounded checks ([alloc.dev](https://alloc.dev/2026/01/09/redstone_from_sat)). The exact-synthesis literature shows the scale: given the circuit topology, a SAT solver found a gate labeling in **0.12 s**, while finding the topology from scratch was intractable ([Haaswijk et al.](https://si2.epfl.ch/~demichel/publications/archive/2020/winston-exact.pdf)). The lesson is to fix the mechanism template, then solve its parameters exactly. Verilog becomes useful, at most, as a front end for the controller logic of large doors, much later. Write the reply to Raymond along those lines. Codex's draft is close, but it should say you already have the simulator, which changes what a compiler pass would be for.

## A sequenced plan that starts with an afternoon census

**Step 0: make search cheap, one or two days.** Add the screening mode and early abort described above. Run four or five kernel processes on the M4. That should reach an estimated **100,000 to 180,000 dev evaluations an hour**, but the figure is unmeasured, so measure it first ([internal notes](<../runs/bench/search_pilot/timing.json>)). Add two scores to `grade()`'s output: the per-tick aperture Hamming distance for broken designs, and a temporal-slack margin for working ones. No kernel changes are needed. The per-tick `door` strings and `changes` already carry the data ([grade.py](<../grader/pdgrader/grade.py>)). Also redirect the kernel's scratch world out of `grader/runtime/`, which was the only workaround the measurement script needed.

**Step 1, the most informative experiment: a neighborhood census of the Fable 70 and the Opus 4×4 240.** Apply every single edit to every cell of each door's bounding box: each allowed block in each orientation and delay, plus deletion. By my estimate that is about 80 states per cell, roughly **5,600 candidates for the Fable 70, or about ten minutes on one process**, and about 19,000 for the 240. Grade each one, record valid or invalid, open and close ticks, the graded distance, and the failure code. Then sample pairs of edits restricted to cells that the trace shows actually change during a cycle. This one dataset answers four questions no amount of reading can settle. Is the working set connected under small edits (the fraction of single edits that keep the door working)? Does the graded distance correlate with how many edits a broken neighbour is from validity? Do improving designs exist one or two edits away? Which blocks are load-bearing? The last answer is the ablation that module mining needs. The same dataset also produces your repair-task corpus, labeled with repair depth, which is exactly the difficulty calibration buyers ask for.

**What result changes the plan.** If more than a few percent of single edits stay valid and pair moves reach faster or smaller doors, the working set is a walkable network. In that case, skip the grammar for now, run Metropolis search with the death penalty plus margin tie-breaks, and expect a hill climber to beat agent designs within a day. If valid neighbours are rare (well under 1%) and the graded distance tracks repair depth, build the STOKE-style two-phase search: synthesis on graded cost, then optimization that may temporarily break the door. If valid neighbours are rare *and* the graded score carries no signal, local search is dead. Go straight to mined macro-moves and LLM-proposed module swaps. If margins fail to predict which designs break under final mode or the 11 placements, drop the margin barrier entirely.

**Step 2: the speed frontier, a few days.** Delay-sweep every one of the 58 verified doors, and run coordinate descent on the 240's 10 repeaters rather than all 1.05 million combinations, which would take about 41 hours on one process. Publish volume-versus-ticks frontiers. Speed is the objective where headroom is clearest. Your best 4×4s take 40 to 90 ticks, while the community claims an entity-free 4×4 that opens in 18 ([references.py](<../grader/pdgrader/references.py>)). Your friend's stated goal, faster doors, is reachable here before any shrinking works.

**Step 3: the head-to-head, one to two weeks.** Run MAP-Elites with mechanism descriptors, seeded with all 58 agent doors, using single edits, pair edits and mined module swaps. Compare it against agent runs and against agent-plus-search at **equal simulator-seconds and equal dollars**, plotting best verified volume and ticks against calls. Then add an LLM as a mutation operator that edits door-generating programs, AlphaEvolve-style, with plain repeated sampling as the mandatory control. The bar to clear is concrete: 63 or 60 at 3×3, since a 7×10 box must lose a whole row or column, on the way to the community's 56.

**Step 4: package, ongoing.** Publish Piston Door Bench with the search baselines and the compute-budget track. Release the repair corpus from step 1 in a hub-compatible format. Before any sales conversation, run the transfer study Codex described: train on door repair, test on unseen mechanisms.

Two risks cut across every step. First, an optimizer will exploit any gap between your harness and real play far more ruthlessly than an agent. Parity with a dedicated server has been checked on only **two small contraptions** ([grader README](<../grader/README.md>)). The likeliest exploits are in your placement procedure (silent placement, then updates in x, y, z order), not in Mojang's physics, so check every record candidate on a dedicated server at all 11 placements. Second, if search reaches 63 at 3×3 cheaply, the eval narrative changes. "Frontier agents lose to a 30-line hill climber at a hundredth of the cost" is then the headline, and the compute-budget track becomes the main track.

## Conclusion

The five threads in the Codex chat look like five projects, but they reduce to one decision: how to spend a verifier that costs a tenth of a second. Codex reasoned as if verification were scarce, which is true in chip design and in its own mental model of "an LLM reasoning manually." It is false for you. Once evaluations are this cheap, the friend's manifold, RoboGrammar's grammar, EDA's flow and Raymond's IR all become choices of *move set and cost function* for a search whose value an afternoon of measurement can settle. The eval-versus-environment question changes the same way. The scarce, sellable thing is no longer "a door task." It is evidence of where frontier models add value on top of brute force: picking mechanisms, writing generators, spending compute well.

The most likely surprise is in the census. If working doors turn out to form a connected network, the agents' 70 is a starting point that a laptop can improve overnight. If they turn out to be isolated islands, the agents' ability to jump between islands is the capability worth measuring, selling and training. Either result is publishable, and either one tells you which of the five ideas to build.
